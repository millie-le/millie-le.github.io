THE REDACTED
STACK.

THE STACK DOES NOT SIMPLY REVEAL.

IT SELECTS. IT REDACTS.

Benjamin Bratton's The Stack: On Software and Sovereignty (2015) proposed a model for understanding this architecture at planetary scale. Six layers, Earth, Cloud, City, Address, Interface, User, each governing distinct forms of sovereignty, each producing particular kinds of visibility and invisibility. Written in a moment of cautious optimism about global connectivity and radical transparency, the model described a planet becoming legible to itself through computation. What Bratton could not fully anticipate, or what the decade since has made undeniable, is that legibility is not distributed equally.

CHAPTER 1 THE CLOUD LAYER

THE DARK CLOUD LOOMS.

The cloud has always loomed. Long before data centres buried themselves in desert landscapes and arctic silence, before fibre optic cables threaded the ocean floor carrying the weight of planetary communication, systems of media and power were already carving the human body into addressable segments. The interface of that era, broadcast media, state television, the managed spectacle, performed the same operation that the cloud performs now. It named you, located you, and told you what you were made of. The technology has changed. The architecture has not.

Figure 3: CIA Aquiline Central Intelligence Agency, Project Aquiline, c.1969. Declassified 2019. CIA Reading Room, Document C06774185.

Bratton proposed that sovereignty is no longer made about infrastructural lines, it is made by them. Aquiline was a small powered glider designed to present bird-like radar, acoustic and visual signatures, built to blend with the adjacent signal environment of living birds. Surveillance disguised as nature. The state operating through invisibility, mimicking the organic world to move undetected through it. What the Aquiline document reveals is not just a piece of Cold War hardware, it is the logic of the cloud made aeronautical. The system that watches you is designed to look like something else entirely.

Figure 4: Trevor Paglen, Landing Sites, 2015.

THE CLOUD HAS A BODY.

Trevor Paglen spent years making this infrastructure physically visible: nautical charts annotated with GPS coordinates, restricted zones, the precise locations where intelligence operations tap the undersea cables that carry the internet across the ocean floor. What is seen is chosen. What is hidden is chosen. This is not a malfunction of the planetary computational system, it is the system working exactly as designed. The cloud presents as weightless and immaterial. Paglen's charts reveal the opposite: tonnes of cable, specific coordinates, restricted areas marked in green ink on official navigation documents. The infrastructure of invisibility has an address. It is here that governance becomes intimate. The algorithm shapes what you see, and in doing so shapes what you want. Restriction produces desire.

It is at these coordinates. It is being read.

Figure 5: Ben Grosser, ScareMail, 2013.

ScareMail is a web browser extension that makes email scary in order to disrupt NSA surveillance. It adds to every new email's signature an algorithmically generated narrative containing a collection of probable NSA search terms, acting as a trap for surveillance programs like PRISM and XKeyscore, forcing them to look at nonsense. Grosser's strategy is counter-redaction through excess: if the algorithm flags keywords, flood every communication with keywords until the system chokes on its own logic. To find everything is to find nothing. ScareMail does not hide in the shadows, it performs maximum visibility as a form of resistance. It is the Streisand Effect weaponised. The cloud's surveillance logic turned against itself, overwhelmed by the very language it was built to detect.

CHAPTER 2 THE INTERFACE LAYER

WE WANT TO █████ WHAT WE CAN'T HAVE.

The question sits uncomfortably at the centre of all of this. Shane Hegde argues that illogical, self-destructive, beautifully inefficient acts of being human exist outside what the system can model or monetise. But the interface has learned to be patient. It does not need to govern every act. It only needs to govern enough of them that the ungoverned ones feel like freedom rather than what they might actually be: the gap the system hasn't closed yet.

Figure 6: Shane Hegde/Air Inc., AI Would Never Smoke a Cigarette With You, 2026.

SO THE BODY RESPONDS.

Not to hide from human eyes, which see the pattern clearly, but to become unreadable to the machine's. The algorithm shapes what you see, and in doing so shapes what you want. But the tools of opacity are not equally distributed. This is an expensive, aspirational object. Its before and after format asks a question The Stack refuses to answer cleanly: who has access to the right to disappear?

De Blauwer's fragmented screaming faces shift the argument inward, to what the Interface layer does to expression, to the voice, to the face when it cannot be whole. Two mouths screaming across a torn seam. You can feel the urgency. Nothing got through cleanly.

AM I SHADOWBANNED?

Figure 9: Jenny Holzer, The Beginning of the War Will Be Secret, 1982.

The shadow ban does not silence you loudly. It removes your voice at a frequency only you cannot hear. You continue speaking. No one receives the signal. Not might be. Not could be. Will be. The system announces its own opacity as fact, in letters large enough to read from the street, on the architecture of the institution itself. This is the planet the Redacted Stack produces, not the transparent, interconnected world that 2015 imagined, but a controlled information environment where what is hidden is as structurally significant as what is shown. The question of who chose what to conceal, and who had no choice at all, remains,"line-through" like most of the important questions, carefully, deliberately, unanswered.
